1 · Verdict and recommendation

You asked which one lot to buy and build on, weighted for gardening, hobby farming, scenery, a good developed neighbourhood, quick access to amenities and safety from flood and storm. Two findings in this edition decide it, and neither was in any earlier version.

Read this before anything else — poultry looks prohibited

The recorded Bar X Ranch declaration of restrictions permits, on any lot, exactly three structures: one single-family dwelling, a garage, and one horse barn of no more than 30 × 25 ft (§3.01). And §3.15 provides that “no livestock of any kind other than house pets of reasonable kind and number may be kept on any Lot”, adding horses as the one express exception, on an acreage formula.

Chickens and ducks are neither house pets nor horses. On the face of the instrument, keeping them would need a written variance from the committee for the structure and a departure from the livestock clause. If poultry is non-negotiable for you, the honest answer is that Bar X Ranch is the wrong subdivision — buy unrestricted acreage in Brazoria County instead. Horses, unusually, are welcome: 1 per 32,670 sq ft, 2 per 42,000 sq ft.

If you buy here anyway, buy Lot 2 — 336 Wagon Wheel Trail W

It sits on Asa silty clay loam, the better of the two soils and genuinely good garden ground; its best building spot is at 28.8 ft, the highest in the portfolio, so it needs a lift of eight inches where the others need three to five feet; and it is the cheapest lot at $45,000.

Getting Lot 2 to build-ready — land, pad, septic and foundation — costs about $65,000. The same exercise is $109,000 on Lot 3, $131,000 on Lot 1 and $139,000 on Lot 4. What you give up is room: 1.00 acre of record, 187 ft wide, and the shortest water frontage of the five.

This is a change from the previous edition, and here is why v3.0 recommended Lot 1 on space and soil. Calculating the pad properly (§22) rather than carrying a generic $12k–$45k range put a $66,000 gap between the two on cost-to-build-ready. Lot 1 remains the better lot to live on — 1.95 acres and bayou frontage against 1.00 acre and a pond corner. The question is now explicit: is the extra 0.95 acre and the bayou worth about $66,000 to you? If yes, buy Lot 1 and this document supports it. If you hesitate, buy Lot 2.

Why the cheapest land is not the answer for you

On price alone Lot 29 wins outright — $37,692 an acre and the only lot asking below the county's appraisal. This edition would recommend it to an investor.

But it sits on Pledger clay: 70% clay, a permeability of 0.21 µm/s, hydrologic group D, and a linear extensibility of 19 — a shrink-swell vertisol. That is the ground that cracks slabs, forces an aerobic septic system, ponds after rain and gardens only in a two-week moisture window. The $10,000 you save on land you give back on the septic and the foundation, and you garden on it for the next thirty years. See §14.

The case for paying up for Lot 1

You garden. Lot 1 is 1.95 acres against Lot 2's 1.00, on the same excellent Asa loam, with mature trees and roughly half its perimeter on Mill Bayou. That is the difference between a vegetable plot and an orchard, and it is the one thing on this list you cannot buy later.

The bill for that: $37,500 more land and about $28,000 more pad. It is also the closest of the five to State Highway 35, at 480 ft, though 1.95 treed acres absorb a good deal of that.

And do not buy the set Five vacant lots cost $5,850–$6,360 a year to hold and return nothing — $17,500–$19,100 over a three-year window, on a portfolio already asking 30.6% above the county's appraised value. The build needs one lot; the other four add carry, not capability.

An appraisal is not a market value, and Texas appraisals commonly lag a rising market. But a 27% premium on one lot and a discount on another, inside the same subdivision and the same flood zone, is a negotiating fact.

How to use this at the negotiating table The five appraisals total $256,180 against a $334,500 ask — a +30.6% premium. The county's appraisal is the number the seller pays tax on and the number that will be quoted back at them. On Lots 1, 2 and 4 there is a documented $31,600 of daylight to argue about. Appraisal-district polygons are maintained for mapping, not for conveyancing, and the county says so. Do not treat either figure as survey-grade — but do notice that on Lot 2 the gap is large enough to matter to a septic layout on a 1-acre lot.

12 · Terrain, slope and how the drawings were made

The next five pages give each lot a true-scale plan with the county's 1-foot LiDAR contours, and a longitudinal section cut along the dotted A–B line from the roadside boundary to the lakeside boundary.

How to read them

  • The plan is rotated so that A–B runs left to right. North is shown by the needle, which therefore points somewhere other than up.
  • Plan and section share the same horizontal scale and the same left-right position, so you can drop a finger down from any point on the plan to the same point on the section.
  • A is the boundary at the street, B the boundary at the water. The profile is continued a short way past both so the road ditch and the water are visible.
  • The section is drawn three times. Once vertically exaggerated so the shape can be seen, with the true slope angles printed on the notable breaks; once at a true 1:1, which is what the ground really looks like; and once as an earth-curvature check.

Where the elevations come from

Contours are Brazoria County's published LiDAR product at a 1-foot interval. The profile is sampled from the USGS 3DEP 1-metre digital elevation model at roughly 1 m spacing along the line, converted from metres to feet. The two are independent datasets and they agree to within about a foot across all five lots, which is the main reason to trust either.

Honest limitation A DEM is a model of a bare-earth surface, not a survey. Slope angles quoted over a 6–8 m baseline are reliable to a fraction of a degree; spot elevations are good to roughly ±0.5 ft. An elevation certificate will be written from an instrument survey, not from this.
Why the aerials are not sharper — and why they have not been upscaled The lot aerials come from USGS NAIP at 30 cm native, the best free public orthoimagery for this area. Esri World Imagery was tested as an alternative and reaches 0.261 m per pixel here — a 15% gain, not a transformation; above zoom 19 its tiles return only 123 distinct colours, which is upsampling rather than new detail. So 2,400 m² of Texas coastal prairie simply does not exist at higher resolution in any free source.

Rather than stretch a 1,400 px frame across the full column at ~198 dpi, or upscale it and invent sharpness the imagery does not contain, each aerial is presented at the width its own pixels support at 300 dpi. That is why the frames on the narrower parcels are smaller on the page: they are sharp at the size shown. The seller's oblique photography in §6 and §10 is 1,280 px and is reproduced three-up, where it prints at about 555 dpi.

  • Texas requires no fishing licence on water wholly enclosed within private property — and where that exemption applies, no state bag or size limits apply either. So the “10 catfish per day” in the original document cannot be state law. It can only be a POA house rule. verified

The flood finding undercuts the fishing premise.

When the bayou runs dry, and what is actually stocked

Appendix D · The seller photography — what was supplied, what was duplicated, and what it proves

Seventy-one image files were supplied for the five lots. They reduce to 42 distinct scenes. This appendix records how that reduction was done, why the highest-pixel-count files were not the ones used, and the one finding that changes how every photograph in this document should be read.

The finding: filenames identify listings, not parcels The files arrive grouped by street address, which invites the assumption that a file named for 808 Wagon Wheel Trail shows 808 Wagon Wheel Trail. It does not follow. Eleven of the 48 de-duplicated frames were supplied under two or three different addresses. The clearest case: a frame showing Flag Lake was filed under the 1127 Saddle Horn Bend listing — and Flag Lake is 1.3 miles from that parcel. The seller shot one set of drone passes over this part of the subdivision and reused the attractive frames across every listing. What was not reused is the annotation. Where a frame carries a drawn boundary or a printed address label, that was added for one specific listing and appears under no other. So the annotation is the evidence of which parcel is shown; the scenery is not.

Every photograph in this document is therefore captioned under one of two labels, and they are printed on the figures themselves:

  • boundary marked — the frame carries the seller's own outline or address label. This is the seller identifying the parcel. It is still the seller's word, not a survey, but it is specific to one listing.
  • area context — no annotation. The frame shows the subdivision, the water or the highway. It is not a picture of the parcel and must not be read as one.

Why the biggest files were not the ones used

Seventeen of the 71 files were exactly 7,680 or 16,000 pixels wide. Those are not camera dimensions; they are the output sizes of an AI super-resolution pass (Real-ESRGAN at 6× and 12×) run over source frames about 1,290 pixels wide. The extra pixels are not detail the camera recorded — they are detail the model invented. This document has already declined to upscale the NAIP aerials for the same reason, and it would be incoherent to accept invented detail here while refusing it there.

The practical point is that nothing was lost by refusing them. Each gallery image is placed at a printed height of 48 mm — the roughly two inches asked for — which needs about 620 pixels. The genuine originals supply 700–1,090 pixels of height, so every placement prints at 328 dpi, above the 300 dpi commercial-print standard. The upscales would have printed the same image at some 3,000 dpi: six times finer than any printer resolves, from a source that never held the information.

How the duplicates were found

By perceptual hash rather than by file checksum, because almost none of these duplicates are byte-identical — they differ in format, in JPEG quality and in pixel dimensions, so a checksum comparison finds nothing. Each image is reduced to greyscale at 17×16, and each pixel is compared with its right-hand neighbour to give a 256-bit difference hash. Two frames are treated as the same when their hashes differ in 22 bits or fewer and their aspect ratios agree within 6%. The aspect test is what stops a genuine crop being discarded as a duplicate of its parent.

One consequence worth stating, because it cuts against the method. Frames that differ only by the seller's annotation sit at a hash distance of about 15 bits — inside the threshold. They were deliberately kept as separate frames: an outlined frame and its unoutlined twin are the same photograph but not the same evidence, and it is precisely the annotation that carries the attribution. Collapsing them would have destroyed the one thing that makes the photography useful.

What to do with this Two things. First, commission a drone pass on any lot you shortlist — it is a few hundred dollars against a six-figure decision, and on Lots 2 and 3 it is the only way to see the ground at all. Second, when the seller or an agent sends you a photograph, ask which parcel it shows and how they know. On this evidence the answer will sometimes be that the frame was shot over a different lot a mile away.
